Case study · Side project · September 2026

Forecasts that have to prove themselves before they're shown.

Language models are bad at numbers. Forecasting models can't read. Foresight pairs them: Claude turns a question like “will Halloween interest peak again this year?” into a real data series, Google's TimesFM forecasts it, and every forecast is scored against a simple baseline before you see it.

01

Two models that fail in opposite ways.

Ask a language model to forecast and it will give you a confident number with nothing behind it. Ask a forecasting model and it needs you to already know which series to forecast and how to fetch it.

Foresight lets each do the part it's good at. Claude reads the question, decides which public data measures it (Wikipedia pageviews, package downloads, crypto prices, weather), fetches it live and explains the result. TimesFM, run locally, produces the forecast and its uncertainty band.

02

Four decisions.

AEvery forecast is backtested on the spot.

Before answering, Foresight hides the most recent stretch of data, forecasts it blind and scores the result against a seasonal-naive baseline. The agent has to ground its confidence in that score, and the score sits under every chart.

LimitIt's one window, a sanity check rather than a full evaluation. It catches a model that's wrong; it can't prove one is right.

BRaw numbers never enter the model's context.

Tools pass series IDs around and hand Claude summaries. Thousands of data points never touch the prompt, which keeps answers cheap and stops the model from doing arithmetic it's bad at.

CostClaude can only reason about what the summaries include, so I added peak dates when it started guessing at timing.

CRun the forecaster locally.

TimesFM runs on a laptop CPU in about a tenth of a second per forecast. No GPU, no forecasting API.

CostA one-gigabyte download on first start, and the 3.0 weights are non-commercial.

DFailures are results.

A failed tool call comes back to Claude as an error it can recover from, instead of crashing the answer.

WhyAn agent that fetches live data will hit bad inputs. It should say so, not invent around them.

03

What it got right, and where it didn't.

QuestionResult
Halloween interestPeak on Oct 31 with 6.2% backtest error; baseline 39.5%
Black FridayPeak on Nov 27, 2026: the right date, not last year's Nov 28
Bitcoin priceA flat median and a widening band: the honest answer for a near-random walk
openai downloads on PyPIMissed: the band covered 20% of days after downloads fell ~40%
News-driven pageviewsNo edge over the baseline

Nobody told the model what Halloween is. From about a thousand undated numbers, it put the peak on the right day. It also knows when to shrug: on news-driven series, it says it has no edge.

04

What I'd do differently.

  • Backtest more than one window. A single recent window can flatter or punish a model by luck.
  • Publish the follow-up. I predicted Halloween in September; the real numbers are the part people would trust most.
  • Concede the obvious objection up front. TimesFM may have seen Wikipedia pageviews in training. Say so before someone else does.
